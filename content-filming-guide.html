RISEFORIT Filming Guide

Film it in
one take.

You already know how to talk to a client on the gym floor. That's the whole skill. Everything below is just making sure the phone doesn't get in the way.

01

Before you hit record

  • Write your bullets. Not a script. Open today's post in the app, look at the beats, jot them on paper or in your notes. Bullets keep you natural. A script makes you sound like you're reading, because you are.
  • Know your first line cold. Say the hook out loud twice before recording. Once the first five seconds are smooth, the rest follows.
  • One idea per video. If you're covering two things, that's two videos. You've just saved yourself a day.
02

Where to stand

Light comes from in front of you, never behind. That's 90% of looking good on camera, and it costs nothing.

  • Film where you actually train. The gym floor beats a clean wall every time. Racks, plates, people in the background — it's proof you're there. A blank room could be anyone.
  • Watch what's behind you. Not a mirror pointed at you, not a doorway with people walking through. Anything moving behind your head pulls the eye off your face.
  • Somewhere you can talk. Music thumping over your voice loses people faster than anything. Step into a corner or a stairwell if you have to.
03

Camera and framing

  • Vertical, always. Reels, TikTok and stories are all vertical. A horizontal video gets cropped and you lose your own head. One exception in your whole program — the video on your coach page. See 07.
  • Phone at eye level. Prop it on a water bottle, a dumbbell, a bench, the edge of a rack. Below eye level films up your nose and nobody looks good from there.
  • Chest up, not head to toe. Close enough that they can read your face. That's where people decide whether they trust you.
  • Look at the lens, not the screen. Easiest fix on this whole page. Looking at yourself reads as looking away from them.
  • Clean the lens. Your phone lives in a pocket. Ten seconds on your shirt and it's sharp again.
04

How to talk

Loses people

Reading a script off the screen.

"Hey guys, so today I wanted to talk about…"

Presenter voice — louder and faster than you actually are.

Rambling, then finding the point at 40 seconds.

Works

Talking to one person, like a client on the floor.

Starting on the hook. No runway, no greeting.

Your normal voice. Blunt, funny, calm — whatever you are.

Point first, then explain it.

Skip the intro. "Hey guys, what's up" costs you the only three seconds that matter. Start on the hook — the first words out of your mouth should be the reason to keep watching.
05

Timing

Your script gives you the beats. Here's roughly where they land in a 60-second video.

TimeWhat's happening
0:00–0:05The hook. Name the group and the problem. No intro, no throat-clearing.
0:05–0:20What it's costing them. Specific.
0:20–0:40The meat. This is you — the part nobody can write for you.
0:40–0:50Name the fix. Don't teach the whole thing.
0:50–1:00The CTA. Same words every time.
Under 30 seconds is too thin. Over 90 loses them. 45 to 60 is the range. If you're running long, you're covering two ideas — cut one and film it tomorrow. That's for your feed. The video on your coach page runs ninety seconds to two minutes on purpose — see 07.
06

Recording and editing

  • Use the Instagram Edits app. You can pause mid-recording and redo just the part you fumbled, instead of starting the whole video over. This is the single biggest time-saver on this page.
  • One take is fine. Do not re-record eight times. The fourth take is always worse than the second — you lose the energy and start performing.
  • Put your hook on screen as text. Most people scroll with the sound off. If the hook is only spoken, they never hear it.
  • Don't over-edit. No zooms every two seconds, no stock clips, no dramatic music. You're teaching, not entertaining. Cuts should remove dead air, nothing else.
  • Caption = your hook, written out, then the CTA. Link in the caption as well as the bio.
07

The one video that breaks these rules

Everything above is for your feed. The video that sits on your coach page — the VSL — has a different job, so it plays by different rules. It's the only one that does.

  • Sideways, not vertical. Your page plays it in a wide frame. Film it upright and you get black bars down both sides. Turn the phone.
  • Ninety seconds to two minutes, not sixty. Ninety is the floor. Nobody reaches your page by accident — they clicked, so they'll give you the time. They're there to find out whether you're legit, what actually happens if they sign up, and whether it's for someone like them. A 45-second version answers none of it.
  • Assume the sound is on. They pressed play on purpose. No text on screen, no captions, no music.
  • Notes just off camera are fine. Nobody can tell, and everybody does it. Ninety seconds is more than anyone holds cold.
  • Film it straight after your pinned videos. Same room, same light, one setup, already warm. It's the fourth video of the session — not the hardest thing you've ever done.
  • Set the camera to 720p before you film. Your page takes files up to 80MB. That's roughly two minutes at 720p, about eighty seconds at 1080p, and under half a minute at 4K — so at anything higher than 720p the upload will refuse a video this long. On an iPhone it's Settings → Camera → Record Video → 720p HD at 30 fps; on Android it's video quality in the camera app's own settings.
  • Then check it played. When you press Done in the editor it saves and publishes in one step. Open your own link on your phone afterwards and watch the video play. If it isn't there, it didn't upload.
Everything else on this page still holds. Light on your face, phone at eye level, chest up, talking to one person, no "hey guys". Only the shape changes. The way you talk doesn't.

Before you post

Three decent videos beat one perfect one that never gets filmed.

Nobody's first fifty are good. Post them anyway — that's how the next fifty get better, and the people watching aren't comparing you to a studio. They're deciding whether they trust you.
